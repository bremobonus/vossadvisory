The bet

A $500 racket is mostly not racket.

Every premium padel racket is the same three things: a carbon tube frame, a die-cut EVA or foam core, and two thin composite faces, pressed in a steel mould. The materials are a small fraction of the shelf price. The rest is tooling, pro contracts, distributors, retail margin and a new paint job every season.

01

No moulds

A printer does not care what shape it prints. No steel tooling to amortise, no minimum order, and a geometry change is a file update, not a new mould.

02

The shell is the mould

The printed frame and bonded skins form a closed cavity. Foam is poured in and cures in place, bonding to everything it touches. One step replaces cutting, laying up and pressing.

03

One model, sold direct

One racket, one price, no distributor and no retailer. Feel is tuned inside the racket (core zones, trim weights), so there is no ten-model range to stock.

Architecture

Six parts. One print farm.

Fig. 1 · exploded stacknot to scale in Z

  1. Face skin A

    0.5 mm pre-cured carbon/glass plate, routed to shape. Carbon carries the in-plane load that printed plastic cannot; this is the one place we refuse to print.

  2. Zoned foam core

    Flexible polyurethane foam, about 65 kg/m³, metered into the closed shell. Thin printed baffles split the cavity into three zones so the sweet spot can be softer than the perimeter.

  3. Printed exo-frame

    PA12-CF head ring, 0.9 mm outer wall with internal ribs, printed flat so the fibre-filled roads run around the hoop. Fits a 300 mm-class bed.

  4. Throat, handle and twin spars

    Printed separately and joined to the head over two 6 × 4 mm pultruded carbon tubes that run 300 mm from the butt cap into the head. The spars carry the bending at the neck, the weakest point of any printed part.

  5. Face skin B

    Identical to A. Both skins are bonded to the frame before the pour, so the foam locks them from the inside.

  6. TPU bumper and trim pockets

    A printed TPU edge guard takes wall strikes. Two pockets (12 o'clock and butt cap) accept 2 to 5 g weights to trim balance at the factory or at home.

Where we match

Weight, balance, 38 mm profile, shape and hole pattern are geometry. A printer hits those as easily as a mould does, and the trim pockets copy the one adjustability feature NOX sells at the top of its range.

Where we are behind

Stiffness. Printed PA12-CF is roughly 6 to 8 GPa; a woven carbon laminate is 50 GPa or more. The spars and bonded skins exist to close that gap. Expect a softer, control-biased feel in v1, not a Tapia-stiff cannon.

Where we can lead

A core that changes across the face. Die-cut EVA is one density everywhere. Zoned foam now, and a graded lattice later, let us widen the sweet spot in a way a pressed sandwich cannot.

Unit economics

How $99 works.

Estimated cost of one racket at about 1,000 units a month, built on an in-house farm of 300 to 350 mm enclosed printers.

At roughly 11 print hours per racket, one printer makes two a day. A thousand a month needs about twenty machines, and the capital is tens of thousands of dollars, not a mould programme.

After shipping, payment fees and a warranty reserve, each racket leaves about $40 to cover marketing and overhead. That is thin, and it only holds if labour stays under half an hour per racket.

What could break it

Unproven until tested.

Nothing here has been printed yet. These are the ways it fails, in the order we expect to meet them.

Neck and frame crackingLayer lines are the weak axis. Wall strikes and mishits load them directly. Mitigation: spars, flat print orientation, TPU bumper. Test: drop and wall-strike rig.
Too soft, too deadLow frame modulus plus PU foam could play like a beginner racket. Mitigation: skin lay-up and core density are both adjustable. Test: face deflection and ball rebound against the benchmark rackets.
Pour consistencyA few grams of foam moves weight and balance, and pour pressure can bow the skins. Mitigation: metered dispensing and a clamp fixture. Fallback: die-cut EVA.
Heat and moistureA racket lives in a hot car. PLA and PETG are ruled out for that reason. PA12-CF tolerates it, but it must be dried before printing and checked for creep at 70 °C.
Skin delaminationThe skin-to-frame bond sees every impact. Mitigation: wide bonding flange on the frame, primer, and foam locking the skin from inside. Test: 10,000-impact fatigue.
Labour creepThe price dies if assembly takes an hour. Every manual step (bonding, pouring, drilling, finishing) needs a jig before launch.

Route to a racket you can hit with

P0 · 3 weeks

Buy and measure

Buy one NOX AT10 18K and one Pallap Elite. Measure weight, balance, swing weight, face deflection and rebound. That becomes the target sheet.

P1 · 6 weeks

Print and pour

CAD the exo-frame, print ten, pour three core densities. Weigh everything against the mass budget.

P2 · 6 weeks

Break them

Fatigue, wall strike, heat soak. Fix what cracks. Blind court test against the benchmarks with club players.

P3 · 8 weeks

Pilot 100

Jigs, metered pour, timed assembly. Confirm cost per racket, then apply for approval and open orders.
